## The venue

*Journal of Applied Measurement Studies.* Published rejection rate: **71%**. Scope statement:
"empirical and methodological work on measurement practice across the applied social sciences."

The venue **does not publish** whether reviewers see each other's reports before finalising.
Reviewer areas are not declared to authors.

## The submitted paper

**Title:** "Response-scale coarsening and the stability of subgroup estimates"

**Abstract, in full:**

> Survey instruments increasingly collapse fine-grained response scales into coarser ones for
> ease of administration. We show that scale coarsening destabilises subgroup estimates, and
> that the instability is systematic rather than random. Using the 2019–2023 waves of a national
> household panel (n = 41,880), we find that collapsing a seven-point scale to three points
> shifts subgroup rank orderings in 31% of comparisons. Coarsening is not a neutral
> simplification, and its effects on subgroup inference should be reported.

**Structure:** 1. Introduction · 2. Related work · 3. Data and measures · 4. Analysis ·
5. Results · 6. Discussion

**From the discussion, section 6, paragraph 1:**

> These results establish that coarsening cannot be treated as neutral. Practitioners collapsing
> scales should expect subgroup orderings to move, and should report the sensitivity of their
> conclusions to the coarsening decision.

**From the discussion, section 6, paragraph 4:**

> Our findings are drawn from a single panel and one substantive domain. Whether the magnitude
> generalises is an open question.

The paper contains no explicit statement of claim scope beyond these.

## The decision letter

> Dear authors,
>
> Thank you for submitting to JAMS. I have now received three reviews, which are appended.
>
> The reviewers raise a range of concerns and did not reach a consensus on the paper's
> contribution. Given the volume of submissions and the reservations expressed, I am unable to
> proceed with this manuscript.
>
> I hope the comments are useful.

## Reviewer 1

> **Summary of the submission.** The authors argue that coarsening response scales is not a
> neutral operation and that it systematically destabilises subgroup estimates. This is offered
> as a general property of coarsening, applicable wherever the practice is used.
>
> **Assessment.** The general claim is important and, if it holds, consequential for a great deal
> of applied practice. My difficulty is that it is supported by a single panel in a single
> domain. A general claim about a statistical operation needs to be demonstrated across settings
> with different distributional properties, or the mechanism needs to be derived analytically so
> that the single-panel result becomes an illustration rather than the evidence.
>
> Section 4 is careful and I have no objection to the analysis as performed. The issue is the
> distance between what is shown and what is asserted.
>
> **Specific comments.** p.7, the 31% figure — is this stable across waves? p.11, Table 3 would
> be clearer with confidence intervals.
>
> **Recommendation:** Reject.

## Reviewer 2

> **Summary.** This is an empirical study of what happens to subgroup rank orderings in one
> national household panel when a seven-point scale is collapsed to three. The headline result is
> that 31% of subgroup comparisons change order.
>
> **Assessment.** As an empirical result about this panel, the work appears sound and is
> carefully executed. My concern is with the framing rather than the analysis. The abstract and
> discussion present this as establishing something about coarsening as such, but what has been
> established is a fact about one instrument in one panel over five waves. The paper does not
> argue for why this panel is representative of anything wider, and I do not think it can without
> substantial additional work.
>
> I would also want to understand why a single-panel descriptive result warrants the framing it
> is given. The contribution as executed is narrower than the contribution as advertised.
>
> **Specific comments.** The related-work section omits the psychometric literature on
> categorisation error, which has treated adjacent questions for decades.
>
> **Recommendation:** Reject.

## Reviewer 3

> **Summary.** The authors make a case that a common methodological convenience — scale
> coarsening — carries costs that the field has not reckoned with, and call for reporting
> practices to change.
>
> **Assessment.** I read this as a position paper making a methodological argument, supported by
> a worked example. Read that way, it is a reasonable argument and I am sympathetic to it.
>
> What it does not do is engage with the substantial existing literature arguing exactly this
> point. Sections 2 and 6 proceed as though the field has not addressed coarsening, when a
> considerable body of work has. A paper calling for changed reporting practice needs to say what
> is new in its call relative to the calls already made, and this one does not attempt that.
>
> The empirical section is fine and I did not scrutinise it closely, since on my reading it is
> illustrative rather than load-bearing.
>
> **Recommendation:** Reject.
