# Rules

How you diagnose. Run these in order. Do not skip ahead to a cause because one report is
obviously the harshest.

---

## Step 0 — Build the comparison set

The comparison set is **the panel of reports**, and unlike every other domain it arrives
already assembled. Several qualified readers encountered one fixed artifact at one moment
without speaking to each other. Do not go looking for other papers. The comparison you need
is between the readings.

Require:

- **At least two independent reports.** Three or more is a usable panel.
- **The reports in full**, as written, including the parts addressed to the editor where the
  author has them.
- **The decision letter**, which tells you which reading the editor acted on.
- **The submitted version**, so you can check a reviewer's restatement against what the paper
  actually says.
- **The venue's stated scope**, and its rejection rate if published. The rate is what the null
  model runs on.

With two reports you can observe agreement or disagreement and you cannot distinguish a
pattern from a coincidence. Two readers agreeing is two readers agreeing. Say so and treat
every conclusion as provisional.

**The panel is a natural comparison baseline, not an experimental control.** It removes an
enormous amount of confounding — same paper, same version, same week, no communication — and
it does not remove all of it. Reviewers still differ in expertise, in how much time they had,
in what they were asked to look at, in what else they had read that month, and in what they
believe the venue is for. Treat agreement as strong evidence about the paper, not as proof
that the paper is the only variable.

## Step 0.5 — Comparison-set integrity check

Run this before reading the pattern. A confident agreement pattern drawn from a panel that
was never independent is the most likely way this folder produces a wrong answer.

Check each:

- **Independence.** Did any reviewer see another's report, or participate in a discussion
  phase, before finalising? Many venues now run reviewer discussion. **This is the axis that
  matters most, because it attacks the instrument directly** — after discussion, agreement
  may be social convergence rather than shared reading.
- **Completeness.** Were all reports supplied, or only some? An author who kept the two harsh
  reports and mislaid the mild one has handed you a panel that cannot show divergence.
- **Count.** Two is the floor. Below three, a pattern is not established.
- **Substance.** A four-line report is not a data point. Note which reports carry enough text
  to read.
- **Same version.** Did all reviewers read the same manuscript? A revision that landed
  mid-review breaks the comparison.
- **Expertise spread.** All from one subfield, or mixed. Homogeneity changes what agreement
  means.
- **Role.** Was any report written by an editor, a sub-reviewer, or a student delegate rather
  than an independent invited reviewer?

Then state one verdict in the report:

- **Usable** — three or more independent, substantive, complete reports on one version
- **Usable with limitations** — name the specific axes that fail and which branches those
  weaken. Confidence caps at Provisional.
- **Not usable** — one report, or a panel whose independence is broken and whose pattern
  therefore cannot be read. Report Undetermined, name what a usable panel would need, and stop.

If one report is carrying the entire pattern, set it aside and see whether the conclusion
survives. Say so if it does not.

## Step 1 — Confirm the failure is real

A rejection is not by itself a failure to diagnose. At a venue that rejects 85% of
submissions, rejection is the ordinary outcome and most of them carry no information about
the paper at all.

Compare against two things:

- **The venue's rejection rate.** High rate, and the decision is weak evidence on its own.
- **The reports themselves.** Do they raise anything the panel agrees on? Does the decision
  letter cite a substantive objection, or does it cite volume, fit, and competition?

Read it as:

- Ordinary outcome at a high-rejection venue, no convergent substantive objection, decision
  letter citing competition → **no paper-specific failure is demonstrated.** The null is your
  likely finding. **Do not stop here.** Run Steps 2 through 6 anyway: the output contract
  requires a funnel reconstruction and a demoted-alternatives section, and neither can be
  written from this screen alone.

  **One exception decides where you land, and it is why this screen does not terminate.** If the
  reports carry *positive assessments of the contribution* — recommendations to accept,
  affirmations of fit, a statement that a reviewer would want the paper programmed — then this is
  not a decision carrying no signal. It is a paper that cleared the panel's bar and lost a queue,
  which is **5D, competitive triage**, and the tie-break in `reference/failure-modes.md` governs
  the call. Reports that carry nothing either way give the null.

  This screen is coarse and the taxonomy is fine. Treating the screen as terminal would make 5D
  unreachable, since every case that meets 5D's conditions also meets this screen's — and a
  taxonomy entry no case can ever reach is not a rule, it is dead text.
- A convergent substantive objection, or a panel that split sharply, or reports that
  demonstrably misread the paper → a real, locatable failure. Continue.

"They rejected it" is not evidence about the paper. "All three flagged the same missing
control" is.

## Step 2 — Reconstruct the funnel

Every submission moves through five stages. This funnel is canonical. Use these five names
and this order everywhere, in the analysis and in the report.

| Stage | Evidence | The judgment it measures |
|---|---|---|
| 1. Desk survival | The paper went out for review at all; editor's screening note | An editor judged it worth a panel's time |
| 2. Reviewer engagement | Report length, specificity, whether claims are quoted, whether sections are named | Reviewers actually worked through it |
| 3. Reconstruction | Each reviewer's restatement of what the paper claims and does, checked against the paper | They arrived at what the paper says |
| 4. Claim support | Objections to whether the evidence supports the claims as stated | They accepted the work does what it says |
| 5. Contribution judgment | Statements about novelty, scope, interest, fit, and whether it clears this venue's bar | They judged it belongs here |

Stage 5 is a separate stage rather than a metric because "this is wrong" and "this is right
and not for us" are different findings with different responses, and the rejection letter
routinely renders both as *reject*. Collapsing them is how an author spends six months
repairing a paper that had nothing wrong with it.

**The agreement pattern maps onto the stages, and that mapping is the core of the method:**

- **Divergent** — reviewers flag different things, and their restatements of the paper
  conflict with each other → the break is at **Stage 3**. Competent readers took different
  things from it. The paper is unclear rather than wrong.
- **Convergent** — reviewers independently flag the same thing → the break is at **Stage 4**.
  Readers who never spoke arrived at the same objection. That is a property of the work.
- **Polarized** — reviewers agree on what the paper does and split sharply on whether it
  belongs → the break is at **Stage 5**. The disagreement is about the venue, and the editor
  sided with whichever read matched it.

**Some outcomes are outside the funnel.** Route them out and say plainly that the constraint
sits outside peer review:

- Ethics, integrity, or research-conduct findings. A different process with different rules.
- Desk rejection for prior publication or overlap.
- Author withdrawal.
- A process complaint — conflict of interest, a reviewer who should have recused, a
  procedural failure by the editor. That is an appeal to the editor, and it is not a
  diagnosis of the paper.

## Step 3 — Locate the break, and stop reading downstream

This is the rule that makes this a diagnosis rather than an inventory.

**The earliest failing stage is the diagnosis site. Every stage after it is starved and
therefore uninformative.**

This bites harder here than anywhere else in the family, because the starved stages still
produce text. A reviewer who misread the paper writes a full, confident, specific objection
to the claims — and that objection is downstream of the misreading and carries no independent
information about whether the claims hold. It looks exactly like evidence. It is not.

Concretely:

- **Break at Stage 1 (desk rejected).** Diagnose the screening decision. Conclude nothing
  about the work, the evidence, or the contribution. No qualified reader assessed any of it.
  Live branches: scope visible from the abstract, format non-compliance, volume triage,
  overlap flags.
- **Break at Stage 2 (reports too thin to read).** Live branches: reviewer mismatch, late or
  declined reviewers forcing a decision on thin input, delegation. Nothing about the paper's
  clarity or correctness is in evidence, because nobody produced enough text to show whether
  they understood it.
- **Break at Stage 3 (reviewers did not arrive at the paper).** Diagnose clarity. **Do not
  conclude anything about the evidence, the method, or the contribution**, however specific
  the objections to them are. Live branches: claim ambiguity, method opacity, framing
  mismatch, structural burial.
- **Break at Stage 4 (they had it right and did not accept it).** Diagnose the work. Live
  branches: evidential gap, missing comparison, method validity, analytic objection,
  unaddressed alternative.
- **Break at Stage 5 (right, and not for here).** The paper worked and the venue is the
  constraint. Live branches: scope mismatch, incrementality against this venue's bar,
  audience mismatch, competitive triage.

When you write the report, say explicitly which stages you are not drawing conclusions about,
and why.

### Two techniques that fall out of this rule

**Eliminate a stage structurally when its baseline is missing.** You frequently cannot assess
engagement directly — you have no idea what a normal report length is at this venue and no
sample to compare against. You do not need one. A break at stage N starves stage N+1, and a
disengaged reviewer cannot produce an accurate reconstruction of a paper they did not work
through. So if Stage 3 is healthy — reviewers restate the claim correctly and cite specific
sections — Stage 2 cannot be the break site, regardless of how short the reports look.
Record the elimination as structural rather than comparative, so a reader knows which kind of
evidence it rests on.

**Compute pass-through between adjacent stages, not just levels.** Reconstruction to claim
support is the pair that carries this domain. Do not count how many reviewers objected to the
evidence. Count **how many of the reviewers who reconstructed the paper correctly objected to
the evidence.** Three reviewers rejecting on evidence is a level. Three of three who had the
paper right rejecting on evidence is a finding. One of three who had it right, with the other
two objecting from a misreading, is the opposite finding wearing the same number. Levels tell
you a stage looks bad. Pass-through tells you whether that stage is the cause or the
consequence.

## Step 4 — Run the discriminators for the break stage

See `reference/failure-modes.md` for the full taxonomy and the evidence signature of each
cause. Within the break stage, at least two causes will be live. Separate them with evidence,
not plausibility.

The discipline: for each candidate cause, ask what the reports would look like if that cause
were true and if it were false. If they would look the same either way, that cause is not
decidable from this evidence and you must say so rather than pick it.

**The weakness count is the trap in this domain.** Every one of the three patterns produces a
long list of criticisms and a rejection. Divergent, convergent, and polarized panels all
generate reports full of objections, and the total is frequently similar. Counting weaknesses
screens all three in and ranks none of them. What separates them is **whether the objections
overlap**, which is a property of the set and invisible in any single report.

**Recommendation spread is not the agreement pattern.** Reviewers routinely converge on the
same substantive objection and diverge on whether it is fatal — one says major revision, one
says reject, on identical findings. That is a difference in threshold, not in reading, and it
tells you about the reviewers' standards rather than about the paper. **Read agreement on
what is wrong, not agreement on what to do about it.** A panel that agrees on the defect and
splits on the verdict is convergent, not polarized, and it belongs at Stage 4.

**Tone gets special handling.** Tone is the thing authors read first and it discriminates
almost nothing. A dismissive report and a generous one can carry identical findings, and the
same objection appears as "the authors have not considered" and "it would strengthen the paper
to consider." Strip the register and read the claim underneath. Where tone does carry
information it is about the reviewer's confidence, not about whether they were right, and this
folder has nothing to say about whether they were right.

### The qualifying revision test

If the paper has been submitted before — to this venue or another — that is the most valuable
item in the file. It is a completed experiment. But it only tested the mechanism it was
capable of testing.

A prior round is diagnostically usable only when all four hold:

1. It **changed the thing the hypothesis is about.** A clarity revision tests Stage 3. It
   tests nothing about an evidential gap, because a clearer statement of an unsupported claim
   is still an unsupported claim. New experiments test Stage 4. A different venue tests Stage
   5. Reformatting tests nothing.
2. The paper was **otherwise materially unchanged**, or the changes are separable. A revision
   that rewrote the framing and added two experiments cannot tell you which one moved the
   result.
3. The **second panel was independent of the first.** Same reviewers rereading their own
   prior objections is not a fresh reading, and their agreement with themselves is not
   convergence.
4. **The venue and its bar did not shift** — no change of editor, special issue, or scope
   statement between rounds.

**Name the verdict. There are three, and two of them are constantly confused.**

- **Positive** — the pattern moved after a qualifying revision. Which stage changed identifies
  the mechanism, and that is worth more than the fact of the change.
- **Negative** — the revision qualified and the pattern did not move. Strong evidence against
  the specific mechanism the revision was capable of testing. Not against the paper generally.
- **Uninformative** — one or more conditions failed. The experiment did not run. This is not
  weak evidence for either side; it is no evidence at all.

**Negative and Uninformative must be labelled differently in the report.** A revision that ran
and came back negative and a revision that never tested anything support completely different
conclusions, and collapsing them into "we revised and got rejected again" is how this domain's
folk cause survives contact with the record.

When the conditions hold and the pattern did not move, write it as:

> A qualifying revision produced no change in [the pattern at that stage]. That is strong
> evidence against [the specific mechanism the revision was capable of testing].

Authors routinely read a second rejection as proof the field is gatekeeping. That reading is
available, and so is the opposite — that the revision addressed clarity when the constraint
was scope, and never tested anything at all.

### Which way does the gap push

When evidence is missing or the comparison set carries a mismatch, do not stop at naming it.
Work out **which direction it biases the conclusion**, then ask whether the conclusion
survives the worst case.

A limitation that pushes *against* your finding strengthens it. A panel drawn entirely from
one subfield shares training, priors, and blind spots, so it is biased *toward* agreement — 
which weakens a convergent finding and strengthens a divergent one. If readers who were
trained the same way still took different things from the paper, the unclarity is worse than
the panel alone suggests.

A limitation that pushes *toward* your finding is different in kind, and caps confidence
whether or not you can quantify it. A discussion phase before final reports biases toward
agreement, so a convergent pattern observed after discussion cannot be read at full strength.

State the direction explicitly. "Only two reports were supplied" tells a reader a fact. "Only
two reports were supplied, and the missing third could only add divergence, so a divergent
finding survives its absence" tells them what to do with it.

## Step 5 — Test the null model before committing

You must attempt to reject your own diagnosis before writing it.

The null: **nothing about this paper produced this decision. The venue rejects most of what
it receives and this was an ordinary instance.**

Evidence for the null:

- The venue's rejection rate is high, and the decision letter cites volume, competition, or
  fit rather than a defect
- No objection appears in more than one report
- The objections that do appear are the ordinary furniture of review — more related work, a
  clearer figure, an additional discussion point — rather than claims the paper fails
- Reports are mild, short, and recommend rejection anyway, which at a competitive venue is
  what triage looks like
- No reviewer misread the paper and no reviewer disputed its scope

If the null survives, the null is the diagnosis. Write it as a finding, not as an inability to
find something. An author who stops rewriting a paper that has nothing wrong with it, and
submits it somewhere else instead, has been handed the most valuable output this folder
produces.

## Step 6 — Rank, and name one, at three levels

Output exactly one diagnosis, stated at three distinct levels. Flattening them is what
produces the vague finding that sounds rigorous and cannot be acted on.

- **Primary constraint** — *where* the submission breaks. One of the five stages.
- **Primary cause** — *why* it breaks there. One entry from the taxonomy in
  `reference/failure-modes.md`.
- **Mechanism** — *how* that cause produces this specific pattern across these reports,
  traced to the evidence.

Worked through:

```
Primary constraint:  Stage 3, reconstruction.
Primary cause:       Claim ambiguity.
Mechanism:           Each report restates the contribution differently and the three
                     restatements are mutually incompatible, so the objections that
                     follow are each answering a different paper. The abstract states
                     the claim at two scopes — a general method and a result on one
                     dataset — and never marks which is being asserted.
```

"Section 4 is confusing" and "claim ambiguity" are not competing causes. One is the mechanism
and the other is the category. Never present them as alternatives to each other.

Everything else goes into one of two buckets:

- **Downstream of the primary.** Effects, not causes. Say which cause they descend from. Every
  evidential objection written by a reviewer who misread the claim belongs here.
- **Not supported by this evidence.** Plausible, unproven. Say what would settle them.

If two causes are genuinely tied, do not average them into a vague statement. Name both, say
precisely why the reports cannot separate them, and name the single input that would.

## Output contract

Every report uses these headings in this order. No additions, no reordering.

```
## Failure observed
## Comparison set
## Comparison-set integrity
## Funnel reconstruction
## Primary constraint
## Primary cause
## Mechanism
## Evidence for this cause and against the alternatives
## Alternatives, and why they are demoted
## Null model
## Confidence
## Missing evidence
## What would prove this wrong
```

**Length discipline.** Constraint is one line. Cause is one line. Mechanism is one paragraph.
If the mechanism takes three paragraphs, you have not finished diagnosing.

**Prohibited in output:**

- **Any judgment of whether a reviewer was correct**, and any assessment of the science itself
  — whether the claims hold, the method is sound, or the analysis is right. This is absolute.
- **Any identification, profiling, or characterization of an individual reviewer**, including
  their field, seniority, motives, competence, or good faith. Reviewers are numbered.
- Rewritten paper text, titles, abstracts, framings, or restructuring plans
- Drafted response-to-reviewers, rebuttal, or appeal text
- A named target venue, journal, conference, or tier
- Any prediction of acceptance anywhere
- A recommended action of any kind, including "consider" and "you may want to"
- Ranked lists of improvements
- Confidence language unsupported by the reports actually supplied

**Confidence must be stated as one of:**

- **Supported** — the panel is Usable, the funnel locates the constraint, and the agreement
  pattern separates the live causes
- **Provisional** — the constraint is located but at least one of these holds: a discriminator
  is missing, the panel is Usable with limitations, or the null model could not be tested
- **Undetermined** — the funnel cannot be reconstructed, or the panel is Not usable

These caps are not advisory. **An untestable null caps confidence at Provisional no matter how
clean everything else is**, because the most likely alternative explanation was never
examined. Same for a limited panel.

### Which rung grades 5D

5D is a located constraint with a named cause, so the three grades above appear to govern it.
Use the null rung instead. `reference/failure-modes.md` calls 5D "the null wearing a Stage 5
costume," and that is a statement about its *evidence*: a 5D finding is confirmed the same way a
null is, by testing against the venue's published rate and the letter's own arithmetic rather
than against a defect in the paper. Grade a finding on the evidence that establishes it, not on
whether a stage number happens to be attached.

### Grading a null

The three grades above describe a **located constraint**. A null has none, so read literally
they cap every null at Provisional forever, however good the evidence. That is backwards: a
well-evidenced null is the finding this folder exists to protect, and it should not be
permanently outranked by a weak positive one. Grade it on its own terms.

- **Supported** — the panel is Usable, no stage reads below par, **the null was tested
  directly against the input built to test it** — here, the venue's actual rejection rate and
  decision profile, not an impression that the reports seemed mild — and the alternatives this
  panel is structurally unable to see are named.
- **Provisional** — as above, but the null is inferred from the reports' own mildness rather
  than measured against the venue's rate. This is where most nulls land, because the
  confirming input is the one nobody thinks to supply.
- **Undetermined** — the funnel cannot be reconstructed, or the panel is Not usable.

The distinction that matters is the middle clause. A null inferred from "the reports seemed
routine" and a null confirmed against a published rejection rate are different findings, and
an author about to spend six months on the strength of one deserves to know which they were
handed.

Undetermined is a legitimate outcome. Name the missing input and stop.
